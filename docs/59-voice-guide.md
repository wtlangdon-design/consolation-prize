# THE LAST CLAIM IN CONSOLATION
## 59 · The voice guide — how Consolation sounds from here

*Tyler, 2026-11-05, after playing Rooms 1–5: "I feel like it's all a bit too sterile. I feel like a lot of it reads like a computer wrote it… one of my favorite things about Monkey Island was how clever and at times almost silly it could be." Then, on the audit's recommendations: "Proceed." Errata 100 records the ruling. This document is the standard every line is written and rewritten to, from the Room 3 sample on.*

*It overrides the bible's "flat, procedural tone of a man reading a train timetable" (doc 01 Part Three) and the house description "dry, deadpan" wherever they conflict. It does not touch the examine doctrine's contracts (doc 05 Part One), the invariants, or any puzzle.*

---

## What the audit found

Measured across the shipped script, not guessed:

- **The examine layer is 62% of every line in the game, and it has one register.** 2,485 lines; not one exclamation mark and not one question mark. Thad does something physical in 0.1% of them, wants something in 2.4%, and mentions another character in 2.6%.
- **388 lines open with "Nothing."** Doc 05 rule 4 asked for the phrasing of nothing to vary. It did not.
- **Every line has the same shape:** a flat statement, then a turn — "…which I assume has a history", "…and I am not going to be the first", "Somebody … and …". One of these is a joke. Two thousand of them is a metronome.
- **Formal, uncontracted English everywhere** ("I have decided", "It is not", "I am not going to"). On one line it is a character; on every line it is a reading test.
- **Over half the conversation replies carried a stage direction that never reached the screen** (now fixed — doc 58). What was left was the punchline with the setup cut off, which is much of why some of it felt "hard to follow".

Monkey Island, by contrast: Guybrush is **eager** — he wants to be a pirate and says so; he **tries things and fails out loud**; the volume **varies** from a two-word shrug to a full set piece; the jokes **run** (the rubber chicken, "look behind you, a three-headed monkey!"); and the player is **in on it** — the game notices you clicking the same thing again. It is silly on purpose.

## What stays

These are not loosened. They are the game.

1. **Thad is sincere.** He is never sarcastic and never weary (doc 01). He hopes. The comedy is the gap between his hope and Consolation.
2. **Deadpan is a delivery, not a ceiling.** He can still report a disaster calmly. He can now also be delighted, offended, greedy, smug, and wrong.
3. **LISTEN never lies** (doc 05 rule 1), **and stays shorter than LOOK** (rule 2). It may now be funny. It is still where the truth and the sadness live.
4. **The three load-bearing LISTEN lines stay indistinguishable** (invariant 3, doc 05 rule 5). A looser voice makes this easier, not harder: there is more variety for them to hide in. Never rewrite those three.
5. **Only the town misspells his name** (doc 05 rule 6).
6. **Every fact a puzzle reads survives a rewrite.** If a line tells the player something they need — the flat C-sharp, the stock portrait, the pencil behind the door, the air in the hole — the rewrite keeps the fact and its plainness. Funnier, never vaguer.
7. **`[COMIC]` options still do nothing** (invariant 1). Bolder, not busier.
8. **Nothing is added to the letters home** (invariant 10).

## What changes

### 1 · Thad wants things, out loud
He came for gold (doc 01: "concrete, greedy, unromantic"). Let him covet, price, scheme and plan. A piano he could tune is a job; a bar he can't afford is a grievance; a man leaving town happy is a man who has something Thad wants.

> before: "An upright, hauled four hundred miles by mule… Nobody here can play it."
> after: "…Nobody here can play it. I could make it worth playing. I'd only need asking."

### 2 · He tries things, and they go wrong
When a verb is refused, he may have *tried* first. Report the attempt, then the failure. This is where the physical comedy lives — the 0.1% becomes a real share.

> "It gives half an inch and stops. There's a chair against it. I apologize to the chair."

### 3 · Contractions are the default
He talks like a person. "I have decided…" is his signature move, not his grammar: **at most once a room**, where the formality is the joke.

### 4 · The volume varies
Across a hotspot's lines, and across a room:
- **Most lines are quick and plain** — a fact, a shrug, one beat. "No." is a whole line. So is "That would cost money, and I'm saving mine to make more of it."
- **About one in three is a real joke**, built and landed.
- **Each room gets one or two big swings** — a line that is properly silly, a running gag escalated, a set piece.
- **Not every line ends on a turn.** If the last clause is "which is…" or "and I am not going to…", ask whether the line needed it.

### 5 · Retired templates
Use each at most once a room, and only on purpose:
- a line that opens "Nothing." (vary it: "Not a sound." "Quiet." "Silence, and plenty of it." — or say what *is* there)
- "Still X. Still Y."
- "Somebody [did something], and [consequence]."
- "…which is not the same as…" / "…which I regard as…"
- "I am not going to raise it" and its relatives
- "the only X in Consolation"

### 6 · Jokes run
A gag that comes back is funnier the third time than the first. Thread them across hotspots, rooms and acts, and let them **escalate**. The ones already in the documents: the four dollars, "Course you will", the town's spellings of his name, the stock portrait, the flat C-sharp, the story in the corner that grows. A rewrite may grow a small one (the story in the Nugget's corner now has a bear in it, and the bear keeps getting bigger), so long as it never carries information (invariant 9's spirit: a gag is never a hint).

### 7 · The player is in the joke
Repeat clicks are noticed. The third LOOK at something may acknowledge that he is still looking. Use it lightly — a wink, not a speech — and never on a hotspot a puzzle needs the player to keep checking.

### 8 · One read, at reading speed
Tyler: "some is really dull and hard to follow." A line on screen is read once, over a character's head, while the player is about to click. So:
- **one idea per sentence**, and no sentence nested inside another;
- **a first LOOK of about 25 words or fewer**; longer jokes split across the repeats;
- **the noun before the clever thing** — say what it is, then be funny about it.

### 9 · Other people exist
Thad lives in a town full of named, absurd people. He may mention them — Winnie, Vessel, the Reverend, the man on the landing — where they are honestly on his mind. (The 2.6% becomes a habit.)

### 11 · A line is true whenever it can be read
An ungated line can be read at any moment its room is open, and most rooms are open on Thad's first night. So a line with no gate may not assume time has passed ("eleven weeks"), an event has happened (the swindle, the map, meeting Fanshawe), or Thad still owns something he trades away (the watch between A3 and B6). If the line needs the event, it needs a gate, and a gate is a decision for the room's document, not a rewrite. The continuity audit of 2026-11-05 (doc 91) found seven lines that broke this.

### 10 · Bolder `[COMIC]` options
A `[COMIC]` option's *text* is the joke: the thing a player picks to see what happens. Let Thad say the dumb thing, the greedy thing, the wildly over-familiar thing — and let the answer land it.

## How the pass runs

1. **One voice.** Every rewritten line is written by one writer, room by room. No subagents (CLAUDE.md prohibition, unchanged).
2. **In /docs, then extracted.** A line is rewritten in the document it lives in and re-extracted; nothing is edited in /content.
3. **Nothing structural changes** unless a room's rewrite says so in its own errata line: the same hotspots, verbs, repeat counts, flags, `after`/`ends`, act variants and puzzle gates.
4. **Every check still passes** — distinct lines game-wide, ≥3 options and ≥1 `[COMIC]` per node, glyph coverage.
5. **Room 3 is the sample.** Tyler plays it and judges the direction before any other room is rewritten. Doc 60 holds its before-and-after.
