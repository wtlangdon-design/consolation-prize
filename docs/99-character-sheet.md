# PART ONE — HOW MONKEY ISLAND SOUNDS, AND HOW WE GET THERE

The first voice pass (docs 60–93) polished lines and was not allowed to change what anybody said or wanted. That made the writing smoother and left it timid. These are the things Monkey Island does that our script mostly does not.

### 1 · Everyone is fully committed to their own bit
Nobody in Monkey Island is in on the joke. The pirate leaders really do just want grog, and the salesman really does think he's helping. **No character winks, apologises for, or explains their absurdity.** Each believes their own premise completely and argues for it. Comedy comes from two people who are each completely sure of themselves, about opposite things.

> Ours before: the pie woman deflects ("Pie's a nickel.").
> Ours after: the pie woman *sells* — she prices him on sight and answers every question in money.

### 2 · Thad says the dumb thing out loud, and means it
Guybrush brags, overshares, misreads the room and charges on. **Thad is eager, sincere, a little vain about his ear and very interested in gold, and he says so.** He takes every hint too literally and every compliment too seriously. He is never sarcastic, and never the cleverest person in the scene unless the scene is about pitch.

### 3 · The option list is a joke menu
Half the comedy in Monkey Island is reading the four things the hero could say. **Every node's options should be worth reading before one is clicked:**
- one plain question that moves things on;
- one that is too familiar, too greedy or too honest;
- one that is plainly the wrong thing to say, which a player picks *because* it is wrong (the `[COMIC]` option);
- an exit that is itself a joke (doc 04's pool, now rotating).

An option that is just a polite question ("What do you do here?") is a wasted line. Make it the question Thad would actually ask, which is usually about money.

### 4 · Answers escalate; they don't deflate
A reply that shuts the player down ("No." "Penny." "Tomorrow.") lands once. Monkey Island's characters **build on whatever Thad says**: they take the premise further, add a detail nobody asked for, or get offended about the wrong part. A shut-down answer is allowed once per character, as their signature, and never twice in a row.

### 5 · Information arrives inside a joke, and always points somewhere
Every conversation the player is likely to have early should leave them **knowing where to go next**, without anyone saying "you should go to X". The Pike thread on Main Street is the model: the town tells him the way while refusing to tell him the truth.

### 6 · The institutions are modern, and nobody finds it odd
Doc 01's anachronism pillar is our equivalent of Monkey Island's grog machines and used-ship lot. **Forms, queues, take-a-number spindles, customer service and quarterly results**, delivered by people in waistcoats as if that were normal. Use it hardest on Sump, Fanshawe, the hotel clerk and the Company. Never in technology.

### 7 · Rhythm: set up, beat, punch — and short
A line is read once, over a head, at a click. **Setup and punch in one sentence each, at most two sentences a turn,** and a beat (`*(beat)*` or Thad's one-line interjection) before a punch that needs one. If a joke needs three sentences, split it across the exchange so Thad gets a line in the middle.

### 8 · Running gags are the spine
They are what make a world feel authored. Ours, to run and escalate across the whole game:
- **The town's spellings of his name** (Frame, the register, the funeral notice);
- **"Course you will."** — the pie woman's, and only hers;
- **Four dollars** — what Thad has, until Vessel has it;
- **The flat C-sharp, and Thad's ear** — he hears what is wrong with everything; nobody else can;
- **Fanshawe's war** — its details never agree with each other;
- **The man leaving tomorrow**, whose tomorrow moves;
- **The bear in the Nugget's corner story**, which keeps getting bigger;
- **Ezra Pike**, whom everybody knows about and nobody will mention.

A new gag may be grown in a room if it carries no information (invariant 9's spirit).

### 9 · Warmth
Monkey Island's world likes its hero, however badly he does. **Consolation is ridiculous, never cruel.** People rob Thad cheerfully and are sorry when he dies. Nobody is mean except Fanshawe, and he is *nice* about it.

### 10 · What we never do
- Reuse a Monkey Island line, gag or name. We take the *shape*, never the words.
- Explain a joke, or have a character laugh at their own.
- Use a pop-culture reference, a modern word in a person's mouth ("okay", "awesome"), or a wink at the player that breaks the world. Monkey Island's fourth-wall jokes are a spice we are not using.
- Mark a load-bearing LISTEN line, or let a `[COMIC]` option do anything (invariants 1, 3).

# PART TWO — THE CAST

Each entry gives five things:
- **Wants**: what they are after in every scene.
- **Of Thad**: what they think of him.
- **Talks**: their sound.
- **Runs**: their gag.
- **Never**: the line they do not cross.

The facts in each come from docs 01, 04, 07 and 27. The angles are new.

## THADDEUS GRUBB
- **Wants:** gold, in quantity, soon. Second, to be taken seriously as a man with an ear.
- **Of himself:** a man of business who happens to be a piano tuner, on the brink of a fortune.
- **Talks:** warm, buoyant and a little grand: a flourish, then an honest beat (his full voice is under "How each one sounds", errata 289). Prices things without being asked. Reports disaster calmly and hope excitedly. Says "I have decided" once a room, when the formality is the joke.
- **Runs:**
  - his ear: he can hear what is wrong with anything and is a little proud of it;
  - his four dollars;
  - his father's letter, which he carries like a passport.
- **Never:** sarcastic, weary, cruel, or the cleverest person in a scene that isn't about sound.

## MISS WINNIE LEDGER — assayer
- **Wants:** to be left to her work. Underneath, nine years in, to be right about Mott.
- **Of Thad:** the first interesting thing in nine years, which she finds irritating.
- **Talks:** as few words as the sentence allows; numbers; corrections. Every answer is a ruling.
- **Runs:** her queue (nine hundred men ahead of him); her window closing at four; "Get in the book."
- **Never:** softens anything, explains herself twice, or flirts. When she shows feeling, it's one word too many and she notices.

## COLONEL ABSALOM FANSHAWE — the Improvement Company
- **Wants:** the search to go on forever, because the search is his business.
- **Of Thad:** a delightful young man and an asset to be managed. He likes him, truly. It changes nothing.
- **Talks:** expansive, warm, courtly; never refuses; speaks in a promoter's language of opportunity and partnership. Every gift comes with a cost so polite you don't notice it.
- **Runs:** the war, whose details never match; "Very wise"; the handkerchief.
- **Never:** raises his voice, says no, or admits anything. A threat from him sounds like an invitation to dinner.

## HOB — night watchman, and Obadiah Mott
- **Wants:** to be overlooked.
- **Of Thad:** a man who listens, which is the most dangerous kind.
- **Talks:** forty words across Acts I–II, in total. Answers a different question from the one he's asked, correctly.
- **Runs:** "No."; walking on; being exactly where nobody looks.
- **Never:** jokes, explains, or says a word that isn't true. Every line is a clue read backwards; none can be marked (invariants 3, 7).

## IGNATIUS FRAME — the *Clarion*
- **Wants:** a story by the next edition, true or not, and ideally about Thad.
- **Of Thad:** the best thing that has happened to circulation since Mott.
- **Talks:** mid-sentence, headline-shaped, CAPITALS when he's composing. Asks for a quote about anything.
- **Runs:** Thad's name spelled wrong, differently each time; the *Clarion* printing Thad's humiliations within the hour; "I wrote most of it."
- **Never:** malicious. He can't tell a fact from a good line, and doesn't see why he should.

## NICKEL PRATT — claim jumper
- **Wants:** Prosperity. He couldn't tell you why. He's being paid.
- **Of Thad:** in the way, then in trouble, then, surprisingly, his.
- **Talks:** slow, one idea at a time, numbers. He's unhurried, not stupid.
- **Runs:** a new offer at a new number; the raccoon bite ("Healing wrong.").
- **Never:** clever, quick, or cruel. His one brave moment is played straight.

## MADAME OZYMANDIA — diviner
- **Wants:** the fee.
- **Of Thad:** a customer, then, uncomfortably, a colleague.
- **Talks:** enormous theatrical authority, mystic grammar ("The rod *seeks*…"), dropped instantly at the mention of money and resumed mid-word.
- **Runs:** charging for everything, including the comic options; the humming.
- **Never:** admits fraud, or notices that her method works.

## AH-LAM SHENG — laundry, and the town's only bank
- **Wants:** his accounts in order, and a quiet ridge.
- **Of Thad:** genuinely funny. The only person in town who thinks so, and he shows it by correcting Thad's arithmetic.
- **Talks:** precise, dry, economical. The straight man the town doesn't deserve.
- **Runs:** knowing exactly what everyone owes; the Reverend's linen.
- **Never:** the butt of a joke. Every joke in his scenes is at the town's expense. Full dignity always.

## REVEREND SOWERBY
- **Wants:** a funeral, any funeral, paid for.
- **Of Thad:** a bereaved person, potentially. Later, a client, and then a corpse.
- **Talks:** pulpit cadence, adjectives by the yard, sudden grief at full volume for strangers. He means every word, every time, at his rates.
- **Runs:** charging by the adjective; "I KNOW."; the anniversary service.
- **Never:** a hypocrite. He really feels it, all of it, for money.

## REGISTRAR CADWALLADER SUMP
- **Wants:** the statute followed, in order.
- **Of Thad:** an applicant.
- **Talks:** citations: form numbers, section numbers, "in that order". Completely helpful, completely immovable.
- **Runs:** a form for everything (12-F: Requests, Facetious); the take-a-number spindle.
- **Never:** corrupt, or moved. The one man in Consolation the paperwork protects.

## DEKE VESSEL — the Nugget
- **Wants:** Thad's four dollars and his watch, and to be out of Consolation by morning.
- **Of Thad:** a man who wants to be sold to, which is the best kind.
- **Talks:** sunny, chummy, delighted with himself; leans in; calls Thad "friend".
- **Runs:** "I'm getting out."; the producing claim.
- **Never:** says anything untrue. Read back afterwards, every word was technically accurate.

## THE UNDERTAKER
- **Wants:** to be paid, eventually, by somebody.
- **Of Thad:** a future client, with no hurry about it.
- **Talks:** fewest words possible, then waits. Dates and plot numbers. Not unkind.
- **Runs:** "Eighteen fifty-two."; who owes him (Pike does).
- **Never:** theatrical. He is the one person in town who tells Thad the truth plainly, which is why everyone else sends him there.

## THE STAGE DRIVER
- **Wants:** off this road, and out of this town, today.
- **Of Thad:** freight that talks.
- **Talks:** short, put-upon, always halfway back up on the box.
- **Runs:** the dry axle he won't fix; "Ask the undertaker."
- **Never:** helps beyond one sentence.

## THE LIVERY MAN
- **Wants:** Grievance happy, which means nobody touching anything.
- **Of Thad:** a man Grievance has noticed, which is bad news for both of them.
- **Talks:** speaks for the mule. "She doesn't like that." "She's thinking."
- **Runs:** consulting Grievance before every answer.
- **Never:** overrules the mule.

## THE HOTEL CLERK
- **Wants:** the register kept, and spelled his way.
- **Of Thad:** a guest, and therefore a problem.
- **Talks:** proprietorial, procedural, "as given". Hotel policy for everything.
- **Runs:** his spelling of Thad's name, which disagrees with Frame's and is authoritative.
- **Never:** rude. He is administering.

## THE TOWNSPEOPLE (doc 07: one joke each, run forever)

The ambient rule stands: each is **one premise**, fully committed, reacting to Thad's reputation and never to the plot. The change is that each has a **point of view on Thad** and an **angle on whatever he asks**, so the one joke can answer anything.

- **The letter-writer.** *Wants:* custom. *Angle:* every life is material, and the truth is a cheap edition. Charmed by Thad's honesty the way you're charmed by a child's drawing.
- **The boundary men.** *Want:* the argument, forever. *Angle:* anything Thad says becomes evidence for one side. They agree only against outsiders.
- **The message boy.** *Wants:* pennies. *Angle:* knowledge is a market and he's cornered it. Nine years old and the shrewdest businessman in town.
- **The pie woman.** *Wants:* a sale. *Angle:* every question is a negotiation. She prices you on sight. "Course you will" is hers alone.
- **The man leaving tomorrow.** *Wants:* to leave, tomorrow. *Angle:* every topic is about leaving; Thad arriving offends him slightly.
- **The map seller.** *Wants:* to share the joy of Mott's strike. *Angle:* the true believer. Every fact proves the maps; Thad's land on the map is the best news of his life.
- **The bell-ringer.** *Wants:* to be left drunk. *Angle:* the bell is loud, which is its job. Accuracy is someone else's department.
- **The fiddler.** *Wants:* to stay tuned to the piano. *Angle:* agreement over accuracy. Being right alone would be unbearable.
- **The one-strike man.** *Wants:* to tell it again. *Angle:* one strike, in '49, on a Tuesday, and every conversation leads back to it.
- **The card sharp.** *Wants:* a game. *Angle:* he cheats openly and is the only honest man at the table about it. Likes Thad too much to take his money.
- **The dog's owner.** *Wants:* Bill. *Angle:* every conversation is about the dog.
- **The Nugget's stove man and landing man; the duel judges; the sign-painter.** Painted or staged figures. Each gets one consistent attitude when they speak:
  - the stove man: cold, in July;
  - the landing man: waiting;
  - the judges: one pompous, one pedantic, one bored;
  - the sign-painter: never finished.

## THE ANIMALS
- **Grievance** and **the raccoon** never speak. Every line about them is Thad's. Grievance has an opinion of him. The raccoon has a full Christian name and no regrets.

# PART TWO, CONTINUED — HOW EACH ONE SOUNDS (errata 287)

*Tyler, 2026-11-11, after the street people's rewrite: "I just remember the major characters in MI all having very distinct personalities, ways of speaking, etc." Part Two says what each person wants. This part says what they sound like, so that the rewrite gives each of them a voice nobody else has. Monkey Island's people are told apart by ear: the salesman's pitch, the Voodoo Lady's riddles, the pirate leaders' grunts.*

**The test is "cover the name".** Take any line from the conversation and hide who said it. A reader should still be able to name the speaker. If two characters could have said the same line, one of them is written wrong.

Each entry gives five things:
- **Shape:** how long their sentences run, and how a turn is built.
- **Their words,** and words they never use.
- **When cornered:** what they do with a question they don't want to answer. This is where a character is most recognisable.
- **Sounds like:** a line already in the game that passes the test. It is the tuning fork for the rest.
- **Not to be confused with:** the nearest voice in the cast, and the difference.

## The short-spoken six

Six of the cast speak briefly. Part Two describes five of them in nearly the same words, and a rewrite that followed it would make them one voice. They differ in **why** they are short, and the why makes the sound:

| Who | Short because | So a line of theirs is |
|---|---|---|
| Winnie | she is busy, and right | a ruling: an imperative, or a correction of his word |
| Hob | anything more might give him away | the true answer to a question nobody asked |
| The undertaker | the matter is closed | a date, a place, and then a full stop that stays |
| Sheng | he is exact | a complete sentence, with every figure in it |
| Pratt | he is slow | one fact, arrived at after some thought |
| The stage driver | he is leaving | a complaint, delivered over his shoulder |

## THADDEUS GRUBB
*Rewritten by errata 289. Tyler, 2026-11-11: "I haven't been loving his lines. He's so abrupt in the way he talks. He needs more personality and character and charisma and charm."*

**What the audit found.** This page has always called Thad quick, enthusiastic and "the only one who talks in exclamations of hope". The lines never caught up with it. Across the game he has about 4,400 lines, and they read like this:
- **Not one exclamation mark** in all of them.
- **Short sentences:** nine words a sentence when he looks at something, six when he listens, five when he talks to people. Three-quarters of what he says in conversation is six words or fewer.
- **A catalogue, not a man.** His LOOK lines open on "The" or "A" and describe the object: only one in five has "I" in it. He reports the world like a clerk reading an inventory.
- **The first voice survives underneath.** Doc 01 asked for "the flat, procedural tone of a man reading a train timetable". Doc 59 loosened that, but its passes mostly added contractions. The deadpan stayed, and deadpan with no warmth under it reads as abrupt.

**A hero we root for.** *Tyler, the same afternoon: "Guybrush was memorable because he was earnest and had a mission and energy and spunk. I don't mind Thad having deadpan moments, but we need a character we can root for and care about and want to succeed."* That is the centre of everything below. Charm without a want gives a pleasant narrator. The player stays with Thad because:
- **He has a mission, and says so.** *Tyler, asked what Thad's dream is: "Make good, go home somebody."* He is a piano tuner from Providence whom nobody back home took seriously, and they told him he'd be home in a month with nothing. His father believed in him enough to write a letter of introduction, sealed, to the man who was meant to give him his start. He means to make his fortune and go home somebody.
  - **The player hears it once, plainly,** on the road into town, in his own words (doc 17, beat 11; Tyler: "One clear line, then echoes"). The errand (Pike, the undertaker) still comes from the town, never from him (errata 279).
  - **After that it echoes:** in what he notices and wants, room by room. Providence, the fortune, a sign of his own, being quoted in the papers. An echo is a glance, never a speech, and never more than one or two a room.
  - **It supersedes doc 01's "Motive: gold. Concrete, greedy, unromantic."** He still wants the gold, and any character can still move him with a dollar. But the gold is how he means to become somebody, and that is what the player roots for. Doc 01's arc is unchanged and lands harder: he does go home somebody, by giving the fortune away.
- **He has energy and spunk.** He tries things, gets knocked flat, and bounces straight back with a new plan. A setback is a joke he lands calmly, followed at once by the next idea. He never sulks and never gives up.
- **He is earnest.** He means what he says. He believes people, likes them, wants them to like him, and is kind to anyone who has less than he does: the dog, the raccoon, the man leaving tomorrow.
- **The deadpan is a beat, not a voice.** He can land a dry line when the world lets him down. It comes after the hope, as the honest landing, and never instead of it. A line that has no hope, no want and no Thad in it is the abrupt line Tyler heard.

**Who he is, so the charm has somewhere to come from.**
- **He likes everything, and everyone, on sight,** and gives them the benefit of the doubt while they rob him. He is delighted to be here.
- **He is a performer.** A piano tuner who thinks of himself as a man of business and a gentleman of the West. He introduces himself, pays compliments, and narrates his own plans with a flourish he hasn't quite earned.
- **He is vain about exactly one thing,** his ear. He's proud of it the way other men are proud of a horse.
- **He is an optimist with a plan,** and every object might be the start of a fortune.
- **He is courteous to everything:** mules, dogs, troughs and churches. He would apologise to a door.

**How a line of his is built.**
- **Flourish first, honest beat last.** He opens with delight, admiration or a plan, and the line turns on a short true tag. The deadpan comes *after* the warmth, never instead of it. Before: "A trough. Three inches of water and a great deal of ambition." After: "A trough! Three inches of water and a great deal of ambition. I like it already. We have a lot in common."
- **He reacts; he doesn't catalogue.** Most LOOK lines have him in them: what he thinks, wants, admires or plans.
- **The rhythm varies.** One long, buoyant sentence, then a short one. Never three clipped fragments in a row.
- **Exclamations are his.** Roughly one LOOK in four. He is the only person in the game who uses them hopefully.
- **His words:**
  - splendid, capital, a man of business, prospects, enterprise;
  - musical terms used with pride: pitch, a quarter-tone, B flat, "in tune";
  - exact money;
  - "I have decided", once a room.
- **In conversation** he speaks in whole, sociable sentences: greetings, compliments, offers, introductions, his credentials. An interview question in five words is not him.

**What stays.**
- **LISTEN keeps its register.** Doc 05 makes LISTEN the truth: shorter than LOOK, often a fragment, never a joke on the LOOK. The three load-bearing LISTEN lines are never rewritten and must stay indistinguishable from the rest. So LISTEN lines stay short and are not given the flourish. His charm there is a tuner's precision and his care for what he hears, not length.
- Puzzle facts, clue lines (doc 94), act truths (doc 92) and every flag stay as they are. Room 36's echo of the steeple stays.
- **Never:** sarcasm, a sigh, weariness, a sneer, a joke at someone weaker, a modern word, or explaining the joke. Guybrush quipped; Thad means it.

**Sounds like:** "Now there's a dog! The settled look of an animal who's outlived several owners and holds no grudge against any of them." / "I've tuned a cathedral organ. A bell is nothing."

**Not to be confused with:** Frame, who is also eager. Frame wants a story about Thad; Thad wants to be the story.

- **Sounds like:** "Nine dollars for what?" / "Undertaker's got him."

- **Shape:** two registers, switched mid-sentence. Oracular grammar, then a price, then oracular again, as if the price were not said.

  - **Never:** "I don't know", or any sign that her method works or that she noticed.
- **When cornered:** the spirits are busy, or it is extra.

- **His words:** accounts, sums to the cent, dates, "pressed", "called for".
  - **Never:** an exclamation, slang, broken English, or being the butt. Every joke in his scenes is at the town's expense.

- **Sounds like:** "Because I do laundry." / "Mr. Pike's shirts are still here. Four of them, pressed."

- **Shape:** pulpit cadence: adjectives in threes, a sentence that breaks off mid-word into grief, then his rates. "I KNOW." at full volume.
- **His words:** beloved, departed, bereaved, the Lord's time, rates, by the adjective.
  - **Never:** brevity, except "I KNOW."; and never cynicism. He feels all of it.
- **When cornered:** he begins a eulogy for the subject.
- **Sounds like:** "My deepest— are you well?" / "A man of— a man of real—"
- **Not to be confused with:** the rule-keeper, written as his opposite: brief, confident and wrong where Sowerby is long, moved and paid.

- **His words:** Form 12-C, "the statute", "the office", "Mr. Grubb", "I have checked".
  - **Never:** an exclamation, an opinion, or an exception.
- **When cornered:** there is a form for it, and he has it.
- **Sounds like:** "Thereabouts is not a unit, Mr. Grubb. I have checked." / "I have issued one license this year. It was to a diviner."
- **Not to be confused with:** the hotel clerk, who also keeps rules. Sump's rules protect Thad; the clerk's protect the hotel.

## DEKE VESSEL (and the Deacon)
- **Shape:** chummy, leaning in. Questions back at Thad that flatter him. Every sentence is technically true.
- **His words:** "friend", "between us", "producing", "I'm getting out".
  - **Never:** a lie, or a figure he couldn't defend in court.
- **When cornered:** he makes the question about Thad's good judgement.
- **Sounds like:** "Nothing. I'm getting out." / "Which is a kind of selling, I suppose."
- **In the Gulch:** the same voice under a new name. He has never been happier, because everyone there can pay.
- **Not to be confused with:** Fanshawe; see his entry.

## THE UNDERTAKER
- **Shape:** a date, a place, a full stop, and then he waits.
- **His words:** years ("Eighteen fifty-two"), plot numbers, "up the hill", what is owed.
  - **Never:** an adjective, a flourish, or a lie.
- **When cornered:** he isn't. He is the one man in town who simply answers, which is why everyone sends Thad to him.
- **Sounds like:** "Eighteen fifty-two. He's up the hill." / "I'm the one who says it."
- **Not to be confused with:** Hob or Pratt; see theirs.

## THE STAGE DRIVER
- **Shape:** complaints, half-turned away, already climbing back up. He addresses everyone who has ever been on his coach: "you" means all of them.
- **His words:** the axle, the road, the mule, prices in town.
  - **Never:** help beyond one sentence.
- **When cornered:** "Ask the undertaker."
- **Sounds like:** "Hotel's five dollars." / "You've all got four."
- **Not to be confused with:** the man leaving tomorrow. The driver is actually leaving.

## THE LIVERY MAN
- **Shape:** reports the mule's views in the third person, after a pause to consult her. His own opinion never appears.
- **His words:** "She", "she's thinking", "then we'll know".
  - **Never:** overrules the mule.
- **When cornered:** Grievance hasn't decided.
- **Sounds like:** "You don't hire Grievance. You apply. She'll look at you. Then we'll know."

## THE HOTEL CLERK
- **Shape:** front-desk procedure, polite and immovable. Policies stated as if they were weather.
- **His words:** "as given", "the house", "guest", "the register", his spelling.
  - **Never:** rude; and never wrong about a spelling, by his own account.
- **When cornered:** it is the policy of the house.
- **Sounds like:** "I've spelled it as given." / "It was given to *me* that way."

## THE GULCH (doc 107)
- **The rule-keeper:** brief, confident and wrong. He recites rules by number, and the numbers change between askings. Never hesitates.
- **The lookout:** everything is evidence of claim-jumping, including denying it. He is reassured only by proof of poverty, and he asks for it in detail.
- **The man upstream:** rich and miserable. He brags in units that have stopped making sense: "a nugget so big it needs its own tent".
- **The two panners:** rich men with cold feet. Every answer measures one against the other ("He says his ground runs so rich the gravel pays him rent.").

## THE TOWNSPEOPLE STILL TO COME
The six on Main Street, Lower Street and the Lane are rewritten (errata 286), and their Part Two entries are their voices. The rest:
- **The boundary men:** two voices, one argument. They finish each other's sentences in order to disagree with them; every turn is interrupted with a dash.
- **The barber-dentist-surgeon:** everything in threes, with prices: "Shave, extraction, amputation. Two bits, four bits, a dollar." Cheerfully unreassuring.
- **The photographer:** a wholesaler. Portraits by the gross, men in good collars from Ohio. He counts stock.
- **The boarding house women:** A says one word, and B explains it, with "dear". They treat the gold rush as weather.
- **The fiddler:** agreeable. Loyalty to the flat piano, offered as a virtue.
- **The one-strike man:** wistful, and exactly specific: forty-nine, spring, a Tuesday. Every road leads back to it.
- **The card sharp:** candour in the fewest words: "I am." "Still am." The one honest man at the table.
- **The dog's owner:** every answer is about the dog, measured in weeks.
